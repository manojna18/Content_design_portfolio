Scout

An AI assistant for style QA, built to catch content drift before it ships.

Role

Content designer

Timeline

8 weeks

Tools

Figma, Claude API, Notion

Deliverable

Prompt system, evaluation rubric

The problem

Style guides don't enforce themselves.

At scale, distributed writing teams drift, and no one notices until the product sounds like it was written by three different people, because it was.

The team needed a way to review content against a style guide quickly and consistently, without making it a manual chore on every review cycle.

The approach

Designing an evaluator, not a linter.

Rather than building a rigid rule-checker, I designed Scout as a structured evaluator: an AI system that reads content against defined criteria and returns a scored, reasoned review.

The core work was in the prompts and the evaluation rubric. The rubric defined what "on-voice" meant across six dimensions. The prompts taught Scout to apply the rubric rather than pattern-match to surface features.

Key decisions

Rubric before prompts

I wrote the evaluation rubric first, treating it as a content design artifact before touching any AI tooling. This forced clarity on what "good" actually meant, which made the prompts tractable. Starting with the AI and reverse-engineering criteria from its outputs would have produced circular definitions.

Scored output, not binary pass/fail

A binary flag tells writers nothing useful. Scout returns a score per dimension with a short rationale, so writers understand why something needs revision, not just that it does. The friction of fixing something drops significantly when the reasoning is already written.

Calibrating against known examples

I tested early prompt drafts against a set of previously reviewed content samples where the "correct" verdict was already established. This revealed where the model was over-correcting for formality and let me tune before Scout encountered real work.

Keeping the system legible

Everything Scout uses, the rubric, the prompt structure, the scoring logic, is documented in plain language so a future writer can update it without understanding AI tooling. The system has to outlast its builder.

Reflections

What I'd carry forward.

The hardest part wasn't the prompts. It was defining what the style guide actually meant in practice, separate from what it said on paper. That gap is where most style drift lives.

Scout works well for flagging obvious misalignments. It's less reliable on nuanced tone calls, which is expected. The goal was never to replace human judgment, just to handle the volume of obvious errors so that human judgment is saved for the interesting cases.
